The Symptom.

Low Pressure and Orange Water.

Most repiping calls in Bellflower start with the same two complaints: water pressure that's noticeably lower than it used to be, and water at the tap that runs orange or brown, especially right after the house has sat unused for a few hours. Both point to galvanized steel supply lines corroding from the inside out, which is exactly what most of Bellflower's original 1950s and 60s tract homes were built with.

Low Water Pressure

Rust and mineral scale narrowing the inside of galvanized pipe.

Orange or Brown Water

Corrosion flaking into the water supply, worse after the house sits unused.

Repeated Pinhole Leaks

Multiple small leaks appearing over time on the same supply run.

Frequent Slab Leaks

Several separate slab leaks is often the sign a full repipe makes more sense than another spot repair.

None of these on their own guarantee you need a full repipe. A single pinhole leak or one area of low pressure is often a targeted fix. It's the combination and the pattern over time that usually tips the decision toward replacing the whole system.

A repipe is also the natural time to address a slab leak that keeps recurring, since the underlying galvanized or copper line is usually the shared cause. An aging water heater connected to the same original lines is worth evaluating at the same time.

The Process.

How a Whole-Home Repipe Works

A full galvanized-to-PEX repipe replaces every supply line from the main shutoff to each fixture. PEX is flexible and considerably more resistant to hard-water corrosion than galvanized steel or copper. Most whole-home repipes in a typical 1,200 to 1,800 square foot Bellflower tract home take one to two days, and we use a manifold system where feasible so each zone gets its own shutoff.

Some homes with 1970s and 80s additions also have polybutylene pipe, which is no longer considered code-compliant. We handle that as part of a full repipe or as its own standalone project.

We work section by section so the house isn't without water for the whole job, and every completed run is pressure-tested before we move to the next zone, not just at the very end.

FAQs.

Common Questions

Does homeowners insurance cover a whole-home repipe?

No, a proactive repipe is considered a home improvement and maintenance decision rather than damage repair, so it's not something a standard policy covers. Damage caused by a pipe that already failed is a separate, sometimes-covered matter.

How do I know if my house still has galvanized pipe?

If it was built during Bellflower's 1950s to 60s tract-home era and hasn't been repiped, there's a real chance the original lines are still galvanized. We can confirm on site.

Do I need to move out during the repipe?

No, most homeowners stay in the house. Water is shut off in sections as we work, and it's typically restored by the end of each day.

Will you patch the walls afterward?

Yes, wall and ceiling access points are patched as part of the job. Paint touch-up is not included but the drywall is left ready for it.

Is a partial repipe an option instead of the whole house?

Yes, for a single bathroom or zone with a specific problem. A full repipe usually makes more sense once multiple sections are failing.

Does a repipe fix low water pressure sitewide, or just at one fixture?

A full repipe addresses pressure loss caused by corrosion throughout the supply system, which is the most common cause of sitewide low pressure in a home this age. A single low-pressure fixture is more often a local clog or a failing valve.

Do you replace the drain lines too, or just supply lines?

A standard repipe covers supply lines only. Drain line replacement is a separate scope, usually only needed if the cast iron drains are also failing, which we check for during the initial assessment.

How disruptive is a repipe to daily life during the project?

More than a typical repair, since walls and ceilings need access, but less than most homeowners expect. Water is restored by the end of each day, and we work zone by zone rather than shutting off the whole house at once where the layout allows it.
