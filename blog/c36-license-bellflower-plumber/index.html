What a CA C-36 License Actually Means When Hiring a Plumber

"Licensed and insured" shows up on every plumbing website, ours included, and it's easy to skim past without knowing what the license actually represents. In California, the license that matters for plumbing work is the C-36, and it's worth understanding what it does and doesn't guarantee before you hire anyone.

What C-36 Actually Is.

A Specific State Classification, Not a Generic Badge.

The C-36 is a specialty contractor license issued by California's Contractors State License Board, one of more than forty specialty "C" classifications the state maintains alongside the broader General Building (B) and General Engineering (A) licenses. Under the California Code of Regulations, a C-36 licensee is authorized to install, maintain, and repair plumbing systems, covering water supply, drainage, waste and vent systems, gas piping, and related work in structures of any type.

Getting one isn't a rubber stamp. Applicants sit for a two-part CSLB exam, a law and business section covering contractor law and safety regulations, plus a trade section testing actual plumbing knowledge, before the state issues the license.

Where It Fits.

The Three License Types Compared.

LicenseScopeTypical Use
A (General Engineering)Fixed works requiring specialized engineering knowledgeRoads, bridges, utility infrastructure
B (General Building)Structures requiring at least two unrelated tradesWhole-building construction and remodels
C-36 (Plumbing)Plumbing systems specificallyWater supply, drainage, gas piping, repairs
How to Actually Verify One.

Don't Just Take the Website's Word For It.

The CSLB maintains a public "Check a License" search tool where anyone can look up a contractor by name, company, or license number. It confirms the classification shown is genuinely C-36, that the license is active rather than expired or suspended, and that the required bond and workers' compensation coverage are current. This takes a couple of minutes and is worth doing before signing anything for a larger job like a repipe or sewer line replacement, not just taking a website's "licensed and insured" line at face value.

What It Doesn't Cover.

Where the License Ends.

A C-36 license authorizes plumbing work specifically, and a licensee is not automatically authorized to self-perform unrelated trades, like electrical or HVAC work, without holding the appropriate additional classification for that trade. Some plumbing-adjacent work, like boiler and hydronic heating systems, actually falls under a separate C-4 classification rather than C-36, so a specific project scope is worth confirming matches the license being shown.

The license also doesn't by itself guarantee the quality of a specific job. It confirms the contractor met the state's baseline requirements to be licensed at all, which is a meaningful floor. It's not a substitute for checking reviews, getting a written estimate, or asking direct questions about a specific repair before work starts.

Why This Matters More on Bigger Jobs.

Permits Tie Back to the License Too.

Larger plumbing projects, a full repipe, a sewer line replacement, or a new gas line, typically require a permit pulled through the city, and a licensed C-36 contractor is the one who is authorized to pull that permit and schedule the required inspection. Unpermitted work on a project that should have had a permit is a real problem at resale time, since it can surface during a home inspection and become a negotiating issue or a disclosure headache for the seller down the road.

A contractor who's hesitant to pull a permit for work that clearly needs one, or who suggests skipping it to save time or cost, is a warning sign worth taking seriously rather than a convenient shortcut.

A Note on Reciprocity.

Out-of-State Experience Doesn't Automatically Transfer.

California's plumbing licensing exam and requirements are its own, and experience or licensing from another state doesn't automatically carry over. A contractor who mentions years of experience elsewhere still needs to hold an actual California C-36 to legally contract plumbing work here, and that's worth confirming directly rather than assuming out-of-state credentials are equivalent.

What Happens If Someone's Unlicensed.

The Risk Isn't Just Theoretical.

Hiring someone unlicensed to perform work that legally requires a C-36 carries real consequences beyond just a lower-quality job. Unlicensed work typically isn't covered by the contractor bond and insurance requirements the state mandates. If something goes wrong, a pipe fails, water damages the property, or someone gets hurt, there's often no meaningful recourse the way there would be with a licensed contractor's bond and insurance backing the work.

Permits also generally can't be pulled by an unlicensed individual for work that requires one, which means the job may end up undocumented and unpermitted even if it's done competently. That becomes a real liability at resale, when unpermitted plumbing work can surface during inspection and complicate the sale.

One Last Thing to Ask.

Who's Actually Doing the Work.

On larger jobs involving multiple technicians, it's reasonable to ask whether the person actually performing the work is a licensed employee of the C-36 holder or a subcontractor, and if a subcontractor, whether they carry their own appropriate license and insurance. A reputable company will answer this directly without hesitation, since it's a normal question and not an unusual one to ask before larger plumbing work begins on your property, whatever the scope of the job actually turns out to be in the end.

The Bottom Line.

It's a Floor, Not a Guarantee, But It's a Real One.

A C-36 license doesn't promise a perfect experience on every job, no license anywhere does. What it does confirm is that the person working on your water and gas lines passed a real state exam, carries the required bond and insurance, and is accountable to a state licensing board if something goes seriously wrong. That's worth the two minutes it takes to verify, especially before a job large enough that mistakes get expensive.
